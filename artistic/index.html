Material study

The last point of contact

Follow one tool from the first bead of silicone to the instant it withdraws. Between those moments, the same connector changes what it carries.

About this mechanism

The early tank prototype used water pressure to push a pre-made child through an elastic opening. This cutaway follows a later proposal. A robot arm deposits silicone around supplied electronics in gel. Switching the connector to air gives the folded shell volume. The pump, reservoirs, battery and people who replenish them are conditions of the birth.

The timing and material movement are choreographed. The project's printing console gives a separate view of the toolpath and joint motion.

Field notes

Who else is there?

Birth brings more than a parent and a child into the frame. These observations shaped where the camera waits and whose movement the studies follow.

Who carries the child?

At Waikīkī Aquarium, a pair of weedy seadragons transferred eggs during a dance. The male carried them under his tail until they hatched. A carrier can enter the story after conception. In the cutaway, follow the arm for a few seconds beyond the end of printing. University of Hawaiʻi ↗

At one sperm whale birth filmed off Dominica, females from two maternal lines took turns lifting the newborn toward the surface. That observation suggests a different framing for a robot birth. Leave space in the shot for someone else to arrive. Project CETI in Science ↗

Where does a body open?

The Surinam toad, Pipa pipa, carries developing young in the skin of her back. They emerge as formed froglets. Her many sites of emergence throw the boat’s single stern opening into relief. The opening a machine has is also a decision someone made about its body. Exploratorium ↗

Where does a gesture come from?

Some Shark Bay bottlenose dolphins learn to shake fish from empty shells by associating with dolphins that already do it. A skill can travel sideways through a group. The relation study gives a child a brief encounter with a neighbor before its own offspring appears. University of Zurich ↗

In Mbendjele BaYaka camps in the Republic of the Congo, researchers observed infants receiving attention from several recurring caregivers. It matters who comes back. A future installation could let a visitor recognize the same unrelated robot returning to a particular child. Chaudhary, Salali and Swanepoel ↗

Who decides what remains?

Robyn Kahukiwa’s 1987 painting Te whenua, te whenua, engari kāore he tūrangawaewae addresses how hospitals that burned placentas disrupted a Māori practice of burying the whenua on ancestral land. The painting raises a question of authority. In this installation, who decides whether the material left in the tank is residue, evidence or part of the work? Te Ara, Aotearoa New Zealand ↗

Three exhibitions in dialogue

Jakob Kudsk Steensen, Wetland. MoMA, New York, October 2026–March 2027. Scanned specimens and wetland recordings give a digital landscape a local history. Here, the prototype in a hand gives the simulated vessel a weight and scale the render alone cannot convey. MoMA ↗

Yuko Mohri, In Concert. Barbican, London, October 2026–January 2027. Air, water and sound leak between circuits in the gallery. For the tank, the equivalent is in the aftermath. A ripple can keep making the release visible once contact has ended. Barbican ↗

Yayoi Kusama, LOVE IS CALLING. ICA Boston, October 2026–March 2027. Visitors share one minute in a room of inflated forms, recorded voice and reflections. Consider two positions at our tank. A visitor touching the copper strip feels a pulse. Someone on the other side may see the hull move first. ICA Boston ↗
