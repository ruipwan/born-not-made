The exhibition

Born, Not Made as a performance in a dark room: from the copy of software, through the births of living beings, to four births of a robot, and two visitors whose view changes.

The idea

AI is now so capable that one day a next generation may follow. As software the answer is easy: a copy. A body cannot be copied that way. A new body has to come from somewhere, and so far only living beings have ever made a new body from their own.

Robots will surely find rules of their own in the end. Until then, life is the one reference there is. So the work studies how many kinds of living beings give birth: what each new generation inherits, and what new thing appears that lets a species keep changing. What is learned goes into a way for a robot to do the same.

The robot is there to serve the idea. The final work takes the form of art: an exhibition with a series of installations, which is also a performance. People witness the whole birth of an intelligent robot. Some are moved, or find the newborn cute. Others find the scene unsettling, or read a warning in it. Someone who walks in sure that nothing in a machine is alive may walk out less sure. The work is an open debate about what we are willing to call birth, and what we are willing to call life.

The story, in five chapters

The piece follows the argument of the work, in the order of the idea above: software can be copied; a body cannot; life is the one reference; so the births of living beings are studied; and what is learned becomes a way for a robot to do the same. The first three chapters are carried by the artist's own lines. In the fourth the visitors start to talk, and two of them carry the rest: one is sure that a machine is only a machine, the other is drawn in. Select a chapter to jump there.

    How the room works

    One thing at a time. The room is almost dark. Only one lamp is up, over the tank where a birth is happening. The visitors follow the light from the smallest tank to the largest, as one group, the way a procession moves from station to station.

    Nothing is taken away. Each newborn stays afloat in the tank after the lamp moves on. By the last beat four of them share the room with the visitors.

    The walls carry the argument. First one mark, copied until the front wall is full. Then, along all three walls, a row of the ways a new body comes out of another in living beings, nine of them, each a small moving drawing with a name, examples, and what the young inherit. When the tanks begin, the wall behind the lit tank shows the study that led to that robot birth, taken from this site. After each robot birth one more place is added at the end of the row of life, drawn with a broken frame and a question mark: the room does not decide whether the robot belongs there. At the end the walls show the Society simulation of this site: many of these bodies, living together.

    Something is allowed to go wrong. In life the new thing that lets a species change begins as an error in the copy. So the piece keeps two errors on purpose. Among the copies on the front wall one mark comes out wrong, and that one is the last to go out. At the end one body does not stay in the picture: the body leaves the wall, crosses the floor as a ring of light and goes out through the open side. A simulation that can only do what was planned has nothing new to pass on.

    The tanks hold the real design. The parent body in each tank is the hull of the Simple version of the simulator, loaded from the same data, at four sizes. The newborn and the arm are still drawn simply here; the full print and birth are in the Printing simulator.

    The setup. A dark room about 14 m wide and 10 m deep. A front wall and two side walls carry one continuous projection, 4.2 m tall; the fourth side is open and people come in there. Four glass tanks on black plinths stand in a row before the front wall, the smallest at the left. A full ring of projection in a round room is a possible variant of the same piece.

    From simple to complex

    Life has many ways to bring a new body out of another. The four acts answer a few of them in order, starting with the least a machine can do, so that the first question arrives early: is this already a birth?

    In lifeWhat happensIn the room
    DivisionOne body becomes two of the same size.Not built. This is the software answer: a copy.
    BuddingA small body grows on the side of a large one and lets go.Act 1. A ready made body is filled and released.
    LayingThe new body leaves early and finishes outside.Act 2. Drawn inside the parent body, pushed out, finished by the water.
    CarryingThe new body forms inside and leaves when able to live alone.Act 3. Printed folded, filled, released, grows while swimming.
    One for manyOne body bears the young for a whole colony.Act 4 and the walls: bodies that bear the next, at scale.

    A lineage you can run

    One rule from life, one from agents. From life: a body changes only at birth, and some changes are errors. From agents: within one life a mind adapts to the context it is in. Joined, the rule for this robot is: adapt within a life, inherit at birth. What the parent body learned from one audience is written into the body of the next newborn. Living beings mostly cannot do that; a robot can.

    Here each show is one birth. Set the audience, then run the show. The parent body reads the room and bends the next body toward that audience: a full room asks for a larger body, people who stand close keep more folds, a tender room is trusted with more parts. On top of that every birth carries a small random error, and now and then a large one. The audience then selects without knowing: the next parent is the body people stayed with longest.

    A solid ring: the body as inherited. An arrow: a change the parent body made for the audience. A jagged mark: an error. A broken outline: a body born from a large error. The bright line is the line of parents. This is a sketch of the rule with made up numbers, not a model of the robot or of evolution.
