Rules

The parts are Bergsonne tiles: sealed, epoxy encapsulated electrical subsystems in a standard surface mount body, all with one pad layout on one I2C/I3C bus, so any tile works in any pocket. There are seven families: Core, Sense, Drive, Link, Power, Store, Display. A real tile is 4 x 4 mm and 1 to 2 mm tall and would sink, so here each rides a float 70 mm square with a thin steel disc under the tile, and the tile is drawn at 16 mm so it can be seen. The dots on a float give its family, one for Core up to seven for Display.

The idea follows von Neumann's kinematic machine, which picks its parts from a lake of parts, and the digital materials of MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, robots assembled from a small set of parts that join and come apart. It also follows Robot Metabolism, machines that grow by taking in parts from around them, and Print & Place, where parts are set into a print while it is being made.

The arm lives in the tail and cannot reach outside the shell. So a robot swims until the float is at its mouth, which sits at the waterline, and stops. The arm reaches forward through the head with an electromagnet, lifts the float in, puts the tile on a rack inside the head and lets the empty float go. The feeder lifts empty floats out from above, and parts left uneaten for four minutes too. Only a robot holding a tail can pick.

What the tiles do. Core is needed to be alive, so no newborn is born without one. Power is needed too; each extra Power raises how much energy a body holds and lowers its idle drain. Sense sets how far it notices floats: 1 m with none, 0.6 m more for each. Drive sets swim speed. Link is a radio: robots with Link that relate share what they see and count each other from farther away. Store sets how many memory traces a body holds and passes on. Display is a small light, so others notice it a little more. Bodies charge in the pools of light.

Two that relate touch mouth to mouth and exchange memory and form. The one with a tail carries and plans the newborn's kit on purpose: Core and Power, then a mix of both parents' kits, then one wish for what it ran short of. If it kept searching, Sense; if it swam far, Drive; if it ran low, Power; if it could not hold its memories, Store; and so on, with drift. It forages for those families, knowing only the floats it can sense or hear of by Link. After a while it takes substitutes, and later it goes with what it has, as long as Core and Power are there. Then it prints the newborn head bottom up. At the layer of each pocket the print pauses, the arm takes a tile from the rack and sets it into the wall, and the next layers seal it in. The head is pushed out through the mouth while filling with air, so it leaves full size, with its tiles in a loose band under the silicone skin. Only heads are born. The tails are a fixed number; after a birth the tail lets go and another head docks it from behind.

A body has one pocket for each tile printed into it. A robot with a tail may change its own hardware: when it has felt a lack, it eats a float of that family, cuts out the tile it needs least and sets the new one in its place, and the shed tile goes back on the water on the same float for someone else. The feeder drops parts at a set rate up to a cap, favouring families that are scarce on the water, and holds a fixed stock of Core tiles. Every body needs one Core, so when the stock is used up the population stops growing. Nobody dies. Every printed body also differs in form: proportions, rings, crest, lobes, clay tone, print texture and how clear the silicone is are inherited and drift.
