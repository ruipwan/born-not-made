Society

Small electronic parts float on the water, each at its true size in a thin collar. They are all there will ever be. Two robots that relate touch mouth to mouth. The one holding a tail carries. It plans what the next one should be able to do, swims to the parts it wants, and its arm lifts them in through the mouth. It prints the newborn head and sets the parts into its wall while printing, then pushes it out full size. The parts decide what each body can do.

Rules

The parts are real small electronic parts at their true size. Core, Store and most Link and Sense parts are Bergsonne style tiles: sealed, on one pad layout and one I2C/I3C bus, T44 is 4 x 4 mm and T48 is 4 x 8 mm, 1 to 2 mm tall. Sense also has camera modules and MEMS microphones, Drive has coin and pager motors, Link has chip antennas, Power has LiPo pouch cells and coin cells, Display has single LEDs. Every part differs a little in size. Each floats in a thin pale collar 3 mm wider than the part on each side, just enough to keep it at the surface, and carries a small steel disc for the electromagnet. A part set into a wall pocket sits flush with the outer skin in a small clear printed window, drawn with a thin pale rim. Parts smaller than 12 mm are drawn at 12 mm across so they can be seen; all others are drawn at true size, so from the whole tank they are nearly invisible. A fixed set is scattered on the water at the start and nothing arrives later; parts shed in swaps go back onto the water. Thin lines and small type mark the part a robot is going for, and soft lights mark eating, touching and placing; they are drawn for the viewer and are not in the tank. The Film camera cuts between the whole tank and what is happening at that moment.

The idea follows von Neumann's kinematic machine, which picks its parts from a lake of parts, and the digital materials of MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, robots assembled from a small set of parts that join and come apart. It also follows Robot Metabolism, machines that grow by taking in parts from around them, and Print & Place, where parts are set into a print while it is being made.

The arm has its base in the tail: a yaw joint turns its working plane toward the target, then two links and a short tool. At rest it lies folded on the floor of the head. A robot swims until the part is at its mouth, which sits at the waterline, and stops. The arm reaches out through the mouth, sets the magnet on the part, lifts it just clear of the water, draws it straight back along the body through the opening, and lays it on a rack on the floor of the head. Every mouth is at least wide enough for the largest part; where a genome's mouth would be smaller, the nose drops less and the snout widens. The arm plans each move so that it and what it holds stay inside the shell. Only a robot holding a tail can pick.

What the parts do. Core is needed to be alive, so no newborn is born without one. Power is needed too; a bigger cell holds more energy, and each extra Power lowers the idle drain. Sense sets how far it notices parts: 1 m with none, 0.6 m more for each. Drive sets swim speed. Link is a radio: robots with Link that relate share what they see and count each other from farther away. Store sets how many memory traces a body holds and passes on. Display is a small light, so others notice it a little more. Bodies charge in the pools of light.

Two that relate touch mouth to mouth and exchange memory and form. The one with a tail carries; when both hold a tail, both carry, each with its own arm and chamber. A carrier plans the newborn's kit on purpose: Core and Power, then a mix of both parents' kits, then one wish for what it ran short of. If it kept searching, Sense; if it swam far, Drive; if it ran low, Power; if it could not hold its memories, Store; and so on, with drift. It forages for those families, knowing only the parts it can sense or hear of by Link. After a while it takes substitutes, and later it goes with what it has, as long as Core and Power are there. Then it prints the newborn head bottom up. At the layer of each pocket the print pauses, the arm takes the part from the rack and sets it in, and the next layers seal it. The printed wall is 4 mm thick, so parts up to 3 mm thick go into pockets in a band around the head; thicker ones, LiPo cells, motors, cameras, sit on an inner ledge low in the head. The head is pushed out through the mouth while filling with air, so it leaves full size. Only heads are born. The tails are a fixed number; after a birth the tail lets go and another head docks it from behind.

A body has one place for each part built into it. A robot with a tail may change its own hardware: when it has felt a lack, it eats a part of that family, cuts out the part it needs least and sets the new one in its place, and the shed part goes back out through the mouth on the same collar, for someone else. Every body needs one Core, so when the Core parts on the water are used up the population stops growing. A carrier that finds none left stops planning newborns, and tells those it touches; after that the robots only touch and swap. Nobody dies. Every printed body also differs in form: proportions, rings, crest, lobes, clay tone, print texture and how clear the silicone is are inherited and drift.
