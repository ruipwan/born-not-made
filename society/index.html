Society

·

Small electronic parts float on the water, each at its true size in a thin collar. These are all the parts there will ever be, and the arm modules too. Two robots that relate touch mouth to mouth. The one holding a tail carries: the carrier plans what the next robot should be able to do, swims to the parts wanted, and the arm lifts them in through the mouth. The carrier prints the newborn head, sets the parts into the wall during the print, then pushes the newborn out full size. The parts decide what each body can do. Tails are made as well: a tail on gel prints a new tail's two segments, and a free tail builds the arm that goes in.

Rules

The parts are real small electronic parts at their true size. Core, Store and most Link and Sense parts are Bergsonne style tiles: sealed, on one pad layout and one I2C/I3C bus, T44 is 4 x 4 mm and T48 is 4 x 8 mm, 1 to 2 mm tall. Sense also has camera modules, MEMS microphones, time of flight distance sensors, temperature and humidity sensors and hall sensors; Drive has coin and pager motors, N20 gear motors with a propeller and micro submersible water pumps; Link has chip antennas, NFC antenna coils and Bluetooth modules; Power has LiPo pouch cells, coin cells and small solar cells; Store has microSD cards; Display has single LEDs, 5 mm LEDs with legs, LED strip segments and small OLED screens. Every part differs a little in size. Each floats in a thin pale collar 3 mm wider than the part on each side, just enough to keep it at the surface, and carries a small steel disc for the electromagnet. A part set into a wall pocket sits flush with the outer skin in a small clear printed window, drawn with a thin pale rim. Parts smaller than 12 mm are drawn at 12 mm across so they can be seen; all others are drawn at true size, so from the whole tank they are nearly invisible. A fixed set is scattered on the water at the start and nothing arrives later; parts shed in swaps go back onto the water. The tank glass is 15 mm thick low iron glass, as the tank of the quilted head study, and the water climbs a few millimetres up each body, a thin bright meniscus at the waterline. Thin lines and small type mark the part a robot is going for, and soft lights mark eating, touching and placing; they are drawn for the viewer and are not in the tank. Short tags give the numbers behind an event: the DexArm's payload (500 g, or 400 g with the pneumatic modules; the holder here is a magnet) and weight (2.4 kg) from the Rotrics specification page, the docking discs (K&J DC2-N52, 12.08 lb, 53.7 N, each on a steel plate) from the K&J page, as recorded in the quilted head and tail copy studies (the K&J figure is not yet checked by a person against that page); the masses of single modules and the latch force are estimates, and the tags say so. The Film camera cuts between the whole tank and what is happening at that moment.

The idea follows von Neumann's kinematic machine, which picks its parts from a lake of parts, and the digital materials of MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, robots assembled from a small set of parts that join and come apart. It also follows Robot Metabolism, machines that grow by taking in parts from around them, and Print & Place, where parts are set into a print while it is being made.

The arm is the Rotrics DexArm of the printing simulator, at true size, with its link meshes, its kinematics and the official joint ranges (base ±110, J1 0 to 110, J2 0 to 85 degrees): the base turns, two 150 mm links swing, a parallel linkage keeps the end effector level, and the end effector turns the tool about its stem (taken here as ±90 degrees). It stands on a fixed base in the tail, 147 mm behind the head joint, and folds there when idle. The bodies are sized after the real design so the arm fits: about 0.8 m long and 0.36 to 0.4 m tall, and the bodies float high, with about 90 mm of water inside, as the real design stands. The arm cannot reach out of the mouth, so a robot swims over a part until the part floats in through the mouth onto the water inside the head, where the arm reaches it. Its tool hangs straight down: the magnet holder of the printing simulator for parts (its inflation needle left off), and the print needle for printing; tools are changed at the folded pose. Eaten parts lie on two raised shelves beside the newborn's place. Every mouth is at least wide enough for the largest part; where a genome's mouth would be smaller, the nose drops less and the snout widens. Only a robot holding a tail can pick.

What the parts do. Core is needed to be alive, so no newborn is born without one. Power is needed too; a bigger cell holds more energy, each extra Power lowers the idle drain, and a solar cell charges the body faster under the lamps. Sense sets how far a body notices parts: 1 m with none, and each sensor adds 0.4 to 0.9 m, a distance sensor or a camera most. Drive sets swim speed: a motor adds a little, a pump most, by jet. Link is a radio: robots with Link that relate share what each sees and count each other as far as the weaker radio reaches, 2 m for a coil, 5 to 6 m for a radio or Bluetooth module. Store sets how many memory traces a body holds and passes on, a microSD card most. Display is light: the more and larger the lights, the farther others notice the body. Bodies charge in the pools of light.

Two that relate touch mouth to mouth and exchange memory and form. The one with a tail carries; when both hold a tail, both carry, each with a separate arm and chamber. A carrier plans the newborn's kit on purpose: Core and Power, then a mix of both parents' kits, then one wish for what the carrier ran short of. After a long search, Sense; after swimming far, Drive; after running low, Power; when memories did not fit, Store; and so on, with drift. The carrier forages for those families, knowing only the parts within sensing range or heard of by Link. After a while the carrier takes substitutes, and later goes with what is at hand, as long as Core and Power are there. Then the carrier prints the newborn head bottom up. The needle goes round the outline of each layer in the clear gel on the head floor, and the fresh layer shows a little brighter. The gel follows the quilted head study, scaled: there it fills the head to a level 100 mm above the floor (a trough 224 mm wide, the water 80.7 mm deep); here the gel takes each head's own inner section, to a depth of 100 mm times the head's height over the study's 330 mm. The water inside stays at 90 mm, as before. At the layer of each pocket the print pauses, the arm takes the part from the rack and sets it in, and the next layers seal it. The printed wall is 4 mm thick, so parts up to 3 mm thick go into pockets in the lower wall, set from above while the print is open there; thicker ones, LiPo cells, motors, cameras, sit on an inner ledge low in the head. The birth follows the quilted head study: keel first and mouth end first. The keel, two tubes along the floor of the newborn, fills first (18.3 kPa there) and straightens the lengthwise folds; the newborn slides out mouth end first and each section opens across only once it is past the lip (the cells at 6.3 kPa, Dragon Skin 30); the rim opens last, and the newborn leaves full size. After a birth the tail lets go and another head docks the tail from behind.

Tails are made too. A tail is two segments: the nose, which carries two sealed pods, a cell and a pump, so the nose can swim alone, and the face, with the seat of the arm printed into the floor and a magnet cuff that docks to a head. When heads wait without a tail and a whole set of arm modules floats on the water, a robot with a tail on gel makes one. Once more heads wait than there are tails, a touch between two robots leads to a new tail before another head. The pods take any pump or motor and any cell. The robot eats a pump and a cell, prints the nose and sets the two into the pods during a pause, prints the face, and pushes each out through the mouth, where each fills with air. Each segment is a quilted double wall of silicone, as in the tail copy study: printed as a lay flat tube, folded three times along and three times across into 18 layers of 4.4 mm, so a segment prints as a low pack on the gel; outside, air opens the tube and pushes out the quilt cells. Nine steel discs ring each cuff. The nose swims to the face and the two close on the magnet cuffs: a new tail without an arm. A tail without gel cannot print, but a tail without gel can pick and build, so the parent then lets go, and the free tail swims on a pump and gathers the four modules of a Rotrics DexArm from the water, base, upper arm, forearm and end effector, and snaps each onto the one before at the open face, in the water, where they weigh almost nothing. The free tail pushes the folded arm, lying on one side, base first into the new tail's face, where the base hooks the seat; the arm stands up, sheds the collars and unfolds. The new tail waits for a head. Arm modules are not born: a finite set floats from the start, each in a foam collar shaped to the module's outline at the waterline, 26 mm of foam round a 4 mm gap and 22 mm thick, so the number of tails can only grow by whole sets, as the number of heads grows by Core parts. The masses of the modules are an estimate: base 1.4 kg, upper arm 0.35 kg, forearm 0.45 kg, end effector 0.2 kg. Nothing is screwed; the modules join by magnets and a spring latch: the magnets close the last 2 mm, then the latch clicks, as in the tail copy study, where the latch is estimated to need 3.0 N. The tail copy study has no collars: there the water stands 270 mm deep and each module has a sealed housing that holds air, trimmed so the base weighs 0.10 N in water. Here the modules float at the surface, so each has a collar. The mouth has a rolled edge where the two skins join, and the docking face carries nine magnet discs, 19.05 mm across and 3.17 mm thick (K&J DC2-N52, the disc of the quilted head study), that hold a tail. Up close, a head shows the quilted double wall of the quilted head study: channels run round the body at a median width of 16.4 mm, as in that study (11 to 18 mm used), their welds staggered row to row; the channel length here, about 110 mm, is drawn by eye. A tail made in the tank keeps the quilted skin, also once docked to a head, clear enough to see the pods, the seat and the arm; the two founders' tails are the hard printed tails, clear resin as in the quilted head study, so the arms show through. The parallel design keeps the water 270 mm deep inside the tail; here the bodies float with about 90 mm inside, so the arm is assembled at the open face instead.

A body has one place for each part built into it. A robot with a tail may change the robot's own hardware: after a lack is felt, the robot eats a part of that family, cuts out the part needed least and sets the new one in that place, and the shed part goes back out through the mouth on the same collar, for someone else. Every body needs one Core and one Power, so when the Core or the Power parts on the water are used up the population stops growing, and the log says which ran out first. A carrier that finds none left stops planning newborns, and tells every robot touched; after that the robots only touch and swap. Nobody dies. Every printed body also differs in form: proportions, rings, crest, lobes, clay tone, print texture and how clear the silicone is are inherited and drift.
